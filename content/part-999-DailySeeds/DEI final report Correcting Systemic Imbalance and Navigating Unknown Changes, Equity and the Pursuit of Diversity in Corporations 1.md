## Introduction

Understanding the systemic discrimination and imbalances hidden within society and organizations is the first step toward achieving true Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI). In this paper, I will focus on two topics: the "paper-throwing activity" conducted in class, and the initiative to increase the percentage of female managers discussed in the guest lecture by Ms. Takeuchi from Tokyo Electron (TEL). By examining these topics through the lens of systemic imbalance, I will reflect on my own perspective regarding the changes that diversity brings to our society and industries.

---

## 1. The Paper-Throwing Activity and the Realization of "Equity"

The activity of throwing paper into a trash can during class was highly impactful and simple, brilliantly visualizing systemic imbalances in society. Although everyone was given the exact same opportunity to throw their paper into the box—representing "equality"—the likelihood of success varied greatly depending on where a person was seated. 

This illustrated that simply providing equal opportunities does not eliminate the pre-existing advantages and disadvantages built into the system. In reality, minorities and individuals with certain backgrounds are structurally placed in seats "farther away from the trash can." Through this activity, I deeply understood why we need **"equity"**—an approach that acknowledges and addresses these different starting positions—rather than just equal opportunity.

---

## 2. TEL’s Promotion of Female Managers as an Equitable Approach

This concept of equity connects directly to Ms. Takeuchi’s point about the current movement in Japan to increase the percentage of female managers to a certain level. At TEL, as a first step to connecting human resource diversity to competitiveness, they are heavily promoting initiatives focused on "Gender," such as addressing the ratio of female employees and managers. 

In the industrial world, which has been male-dominated for a long time, women have structurally been placed in seats far from the target. Therefore, the approach of setting numerical targets to intentionally increase female managers—akin to quotas—can be understood as a necessary corrective measure to ensure equity against historical and systemic discrimination.

---

## 3: The Dilemma of Promoting Diversity and the Perspective of "Thinking of Everyone"

However, I would also like to point out the "new disadvantages and dilemmas" caused by promoting diversity. 
For example, the change of increasing the ratio of female managers might cancel out other positive results that were originally supposed to happen. Specifically, since the number of positions is limited, intentionally promoting women might take away opportunities from men who should have been rightfully promoted based on their performance. A system where "gender"—a factor that cannot be changed by individual effort—becomes an additional point for evaluation, rather than just pure performance and ability, is unreasonable for the people involved. This carries the risk of creating a new systemic minority.
When facing this complex problem, I remember Professor Akaba's words during the Q&A session of Ms. Takeuchi's lecture: "We don't only think ourselves, but also think everyone." Although equity is certainly necessary to correct past systemic discrimination, we must not ignore the sense of loss and conflict of those who suffer disadvantages in the process (in this case, male employees). When promoting diversity, I believe it is essential to always be aware of this dilemma and maintain an inclusive attitude that literally values everyone.

---

## 4: Professional Context in STEM and Practicing "Difficult Conversations" 

This dilemma of creating new structural minorities or reverse discrimination is a highly realistic challenge within my own professional context as a science graduate student, as well as in the industrial sector where I will work in the future. Currently, STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) communities and technical fields, such as the semiconductor industry, still heavily retain a male-dominated structure. When companies like TEL introduce transformations to the workplace—such as forming "DRIVE," a working group for engineers to foster DE&I awareness, or promoting the hiring and development of female engineers—it is inevitable that confusion, frustration, or "friction" will arise among the existing majority, which is men.
How, then, should we overcome this friction and dilemma? This is where the skills for "Carrying out difficult conversations," which we learned in class, become crucial. In Week 5, we learned the importance of "Expect and accept discomfort," "Stay curious," and "Actively listen" when engaging in such dialogues.

  When those with privilege are asked to share it, or when new evaluation metrics are introduced, it is always accompanied by discomfort and conflict. However, instead of running away from this "discomfort," we must maintain curiosity about each other's backgrounds and actively listen with an open mind. Only through this process can the ideal of "thinking of everyone" transform from a theoretical concept into practical action. In the future, when I work as an engineer in an organization, I intend to make this "unafraid attitude toward difficult conversations" my personal guiding principle.

---

## Conclusion

The path to correcting systemic discrimination and realizing equity is never simple. A system designed to uplift someone might bring a new imbalance to someone else. However, that is exactly why we must not look away from this complexity that has no single correct answer. I am convinced that maintaining the attitude of "Expect and accept non-closure" taught by Professor Akaba, and continuously engaging in dialogue to value everyone, is the first real step toward the true diversity we should aim for.
